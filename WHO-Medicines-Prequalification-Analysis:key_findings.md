# Key Findings — WHO Medicines Prequalification Analysis

**Question:** What does the WHO prequalification landscape tell us about
which therapeutic areas, product types, and manufacturers dominate global
medicines prequalification activity, and how has this changed over time?

## Headline KPIs (sample of 248 records)
- **248** prequalified products in the working sample
- **15** distinct therapeutic areas represented
- **2002–2026** prequalification period covered
- **119** listed via full WHO assessment, **65** via abridged assessment,
  **56** via reliance on a stringent reference regulator (Alternative Listing)

## Findings
1. **HIV/AIDS dominates the portfolio.** 134 of 248 products (54%) target
   HIV/AIDS — far ahead of the next largest area, Malaria (23 products,
   9%). This reflects WHO prequalification's historical roots in global
   HIV treatment access programmes (PEPFAR, Global Fund procurement).
2. **A small number of manufacturers account for a large share of
   listings.** Aurobindo Pharma and Macleods Pharmaceuticals each have 25
   prequalified products in the sample — together accounting for ~20% of
   all listings. This points to a concentrated generic-manufacturer base
   supplying WHO-prequalified products.
3. **Activity is not steadily increasing — it spikes around global health
   emergencies.** Prequalification counts jump sharply in 2020 (18
   products) and 2022 (18 products), consistent with COVID-19-related
   regulatory activity (dexamethasone, molnupiravir, nirmatrelvir/ritonavir
   were all prequalified in this window) rather than a smooth year-on-year
   growth trend.
4. **Most products are "Finished Pharmaceutical Products," not
   biotherapeutics.** 218 vs 30 — biologics (insulins, monoclonal
   antibodies like trastuzumab/rituximab) are a small but growing share,
   concentrated in Oncology and Diabetes.

## What this suggests for a regulatory-performance monitoring dashboard
A dashboard built for a body like AMA would need to let a decision-maker
filter by therapeutic area and time period specifically *because* activity
is this uneven — a flat "applications per year" KPI would hide the fact
that surges are event-driven (a new disease outbreak, a new WHO priority
list) rather than steady operational throughput. This is the kind of
insight that shapes what KPIs actually belong on a regulatory-performance
dashboard, rather than assuming a generic set in advance.
